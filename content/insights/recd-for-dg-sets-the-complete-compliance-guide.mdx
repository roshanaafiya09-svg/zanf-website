If you run a diesel generator on an Indian site, at some point a question lands
on your desk in one of two forms. Either an inspector has asked what you have
fitted, or a colleague has forwarded a notification and asked whether it applies
to you. This guide is written for that moment.

It explains the mechanism rather than the deadlines. Dates and thresholds differ
by state and change without much warning — the [official notifications](/compliance/)
are the only safe source for those, and they are linked in full on our
compliance page.

## What a RECD actually is

A Retrofit Emission Control Device is fitted into the exhaust line of a
generator that is already in service. It treats exhaust gas after it leaves the
engine. The engine, the alternator, the fuel system and the control panel are
untouched.

That is the whole point of the word *retrofit*: it is the mechanism by which
plant that was compliant when it was installed can be brought within tightened
limits without being replaced. For a hospital with two fifteen-year-old sets in
a basement plant room, replacement is not a plan. Retrofit is.

The device in a Platino RECD does its work in two stages:

- **FOC — Fuel Oxidation Catalyst.** Carbon monoxide and unburnt hydrocarbons
  are oxidised as the gas passes through a coated substrate.
- **CST — Catalytic Soot Trap.** Particulate matter is captured and then burnt
  off during normal running, which is what "carbon self-clean" refers to.

There is nothing rotating, nothing being dosed, and no filter element to swap
out on a schedule. That matters more than it sounds: a compliance measure that
depends on a consumable arriving on time is a compliance measure with a failure
mode.

## What "type approved" means, precisely

Type approval attaches to the **device design**, not to your individual unit.
The manufacturer submits the design, a designated testing agency — ARAI or
ICAT — tests it under a defined cycle, and the CPCB approves the type for use as
a retrofit emission control device.

For stationary generator sets the cycle is **ISO 8178 5-mode D2**: a
constant-speed test across a set of load points rather than a single convenient
one. That is why the D2 reference appears on the certificate and why it is worth
looking for.

Two practical consequences follow:

1. The certificate that belongs in your file is the one for the **device type
   you have installed** — not a generic brochure.
2. A device that is "as good as" an approved one, without the approval, is not
   a compliance measure. It is a piece of stainless steel in your exhaust line.

## Why the installation decides the outcome

This is the part that vendor literature tends to skip.

A correctly certified device, installed badly, fails on its own terms. Three
things go wrong most often:

**Back pressure.** Every engine manufacturer publishes a permissible exhaust
back-pressure limit. Exceed it and you lose power, gain fuel consumption and
shorten engine life. A properly specified RECD adds no measurable back pressure —
zero as per the ARAI test report — but that is a property of the device *plus*
the duct work it was fitted into. If the exhaust route was already marginal, or
was re-routed carelessly during the retrofit, the number that matters is the one
measured afterwards, not the one on the datasheet.

**Support.** A device hanging off the duct with no designed support is the most
common defect we are called out to correct. It has mass, it sits in a line that
grows and shrinks with temperature, and the load has to go somewhere.

**Instrumentation.** Pressure and temperature taps either side of the device
are what let you demonstrate, later, that it is still working. Without them, the
first sign of a problem is a performance complaint.

## What a compliant installation consists of

In sequence:

1. **Survey.** Exhaust route measured from turbo outlet to termination, existing
   back pressure established, space and access assessed.
2. **Sizing.** Device selected for the rating *and* the duct geometry. A 500 kVA
   set in an awkward basement is not the same job as a 500 kVA set on a roof.
3. **Installation.** Mechanical erection on designed supports, exhaust
   integration, insulation and cladding restored, OBD sensing wired.
4. **Testing.** Back pressure, differential pressure and temperatures recorded
   under load, compared to the design basis, deviations rectified and re-tested.
5. **Commissioning.** Performance demonstrated, documentation assembled,
   operators briefed, warranty registered.

Steps four and five are the ones that get compressed when a job is running late.
They are also the only two that produce evidence.

## The evidence you should be holding

When someone asks, you want to be able to produce, without a phone call:

- the type-approval certificate for the installed device;
- installation photographs, before and after;
- an as-installed sketch of the exhaust route and mounting;
- commissioning test readings — back pressure, ΔP, inlet and outlet
  temperatures, and the load steps they were taken at;
- the OBD calibration record;
- the maintenance record since, with dated readings.

## Common questions we get asked at survey

**"Will it cost us power?"** Not if it is sized and installed correctly. Zero
added back pressure is the tested figure, and it is verified again on your set
after installation.

**"How long will the set be down?"** One to three days per generator for a
typical single set. Multi-set sites are sequenced so standby capability is never
wholly lost.

**"Can you fit it to our make of engine?"** The device goes into the exhaust
line, not onto the engine. Make and model are rarely the constraint; space and
routing usually are.

**"Our set is above 1000 kVA."** Then it is a RATS® after-treatment system
rather than a RECD — same catalytic principle, built to the duct run, and
oriented to the plant room you have.
